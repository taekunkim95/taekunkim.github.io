YOUTUBE PROJECT - Potbad, Fashion YouTube

Potbad is a fashion-focused YouTube channel with over 23,000 subscribers.
It shares fashion-related insights from an editor’s perspective, covering topics like brand history, trends, and cultural context.

The channel is run by a two-person team, including myself. All tasks—planning, research, production, and channel management—are equally divided, except for voice dubbing.
Through the channel, we aim to understand brand strategies, market trends, and consumer behavior.
Click here to visit the channel →
Channel Concept

Potbad is built around the idea of “community”

Viewers often share their thoughts in the comments, creating active discussions.
Topics are chosen based on current events or trends, and we always incorporate the editors’ individual viewpoints.
Rather than delivering neutral information, we present fashion through a timely and opinionated lens.

1st Video Structure & Planning - Why Are Fashion Brands Opening Cafe?

We opened with the buzz-worthy example of Zara’s café in Myeong-dong, using it to grab viewers’ attention.
The video then explores the trend of fashion brands entering the F&B space—not just as lifestyle statements but as strategic moves.
We analyzed these cafés in terms of spatial branding, dwell-time strategy, and entry-level marketing.

This content aimed to highlight the channel’s observational depth and editorial insight.
We chose the topic knowing it resonates with trend-conscious audiences who frequently visit branded cafés.
The video didn't just explain the phenomenon, but also asked how consumers perceive and internalize brand identity through space.

Viewer responses included thoughtful comments like: "I never thought about it that way—really interesting!"
as well as personal stories: "I loved the Vivienne Westwood café in Hong Kong!"

1. Intro (Hook) :
Opened with the Zara café to provoke curiosity.
Asked: “Why are fashion brands opening cafés?”

2. Insight 1
The goal isn’t to profit from coffee.
Cafés serve as accessible, longer-stay brand spaces compared to retail stores.

3. Insight 2
They act as entry points—letting consumers experience the brand at a lower cost.

4. Weak Examples
Some cafés (e.g. Margiela, Zara) lack clear ties to their brand philosophy.

5. Strong Examples
Brands like Aimé Leon Dore and Ralph’s Coffee succeed by aligning space design with identity.

6. Conclusion
Cafés are not just trendy additions but expressions of brand identity.
They should be planned based on brand values—not just as a marketing trend.

2nd Video Structure & Planning - Why Does Japanese Fashion Look Better?

There’s a common notion in Korean fashion communities that Japanese brands are “better”
This video started with the question: Is Japanese fashion really superior?
I drew from personal experience working in a Japanese fashion company and living in Japan to explain the cultural and industry context.

The video encouraged viewers to form their own opinions, not just echo what’s trending on social media.
We approached the topic from multiple angles, including fashion culture, consumer habits, and structural differences in the industry.

Audience responses were diverse:
"I studied abroad in Japan—honestly, not everyone there dresses well!"
"Japanese people really do express themselves uniquely."

1. Intro (Hook)
Began with a familiar phrase: “With that money, I’d buy a Japanese brand.”
Used this to spark curiosity and frame the discussion.

2. Insight 1
“Only the best brands are visible.”
Most Japanese brands known in Korea have already been filtered and validated in Japan.

3. Insight 2
Still, Japan has many strong brands rooted in culture:
Ura-Harajuku, Ivy, vintage reproduction, Popeye-style casual, etc.

4. Insight 3
The bubble-era economy shaped both brand quality and consumer taste.
At the time, luxury imports offered designers strong reference points.

5. Insight 4
The Japanese fashion industry values quality over price competition.

6. Insight 5
Consumers in Japan have less bias toward foreign brands.
Higher offline shopping rates lead to more product-focused, tactile experiences.

7. Summary
Japanese fashion is subculture-driven, while Korean fashion appeals to a broader mainstream audience.

8. Outro
We wrapped up with our editorial view and invited comments.
Korea now has a strong cultural and brand base. Both markets have distinct strengths.
